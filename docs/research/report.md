# Sell Spiritual EQ Presets as Timbre, Not Medicine

The evidence does not support the idea that boosting a "spiritual" frequency makes a melody feel a particular way. The evidence does support the idea that a well-designed EQ curve changes a melody's perceived warmth, brightness and energy. That gap defines the product. The Solfeggio set (174–963 Hz) is a 1999-era numerological invention with no link to medieval chant. The 432 Hz and 528 Hz health studies are small pilots that retuned whole pieces of music, which an EQ cannot do. The Schumann resonance is an electromagnetic phenomenon at 7.83 Hz, below the range of hearing. Binaural beats are a real illusion, but they need headphones and a tone generator, and the "brainwave entrainment" mechanism fails in most EEG studies. What does work is psychoacoustic. A broad, low-gain EQ tilt moves the spectral centroid, and that reliably shifts perceived arousal: darker sounds calmer, brighter sounds more energetic. Valence responds less predictably. The commercial gap is real: researchers found **no parametric EQ marketed with Solfeggio or 432 presets**, only retuners (free to about $30) and tone generators. The audience is large and concentrated among YouTube and Spotify wellness creators. The binding constraint is legal, not technical. In the US, UK and EU, any claim that the plugin reduces stress, improves sleep or heals needs randomised-controlled-trial evidence that does not exist. A producer can safely build a JUCE 9 / VST3 / AU EQ with frequency-named presets. Its copy has to describe sound character and cultural lineage, never effects on the body.

## Every "spiritual frequency" rests on numerology or pilot studies

The Solfeggio frequencies are modern. Leonard Horowitz's 1999 book *Healing Codes for the Biological Apocalypse* popularised them, crediting Joseph Puleo with six numbers (396, 417, 528, 639, 741, 852 Hz). Puleo is said to have derived them by digit-reducing verse numbers in the Book of Numbers, though accounts of his method conflict ([Skeptoid #474](https://skeptoid.com/episodes/474); [Glasgow Guardian](https://glasgowguardian.co.uk/2023/03/29/solfeggio-frequencies-and-making-a-quick-buck/)). **174, 285 and 963 Hz were added later, and no documented rationale was found for them** (Medium confidence) ([OmConscious guide](https://omconscious.pages.dev/guides/solfeggio-frequencies-what-the-science-shows)). Guido d'Arezzo's 11th-century solmization borrowed syllables from the hymn *Ut queant laxis* to name relative scale steps, not absolute frequencies. "Other than the name, the two concepts are unrelated" (High confidence) ([Skeptoid #474](https://skeptoid.com/episodes/474)). Science Feedback rated the claim that Solfeggio tones "remove toxins" as unsupported ([Science Feedback](https://science.feedback.org/review/no-evidence-support-claim-solfeggio-frequencies-remove-toxins-from-body/)). Calling a preset an "ancient Gregorian frequency" would therefore be historically false. "Based on the popular modern Solfeggio number set" is accurate.

The 528 Hz evidence base is one tiny study. Akimoto et al. (2018) played the same piano music tuned to 528 Hz and to 440 Hz to **9 adults, 8 of them women**, on separate days. The 528 condition alone showed falls in salivary cortisol and rises in oxytocin ([SCIRP](https://www.scirp.org/journal/PaperInformation.aspx?paperID=87146)). The design compares within-condition change scores rather than testing 528 against 440 directly. It has not been replicated. The authors themselves write that claimed Solfeggio effects have no scientific basis (Medium; quoted via search summary). A 2019 rat study used 528 Hz at 100 dB ([PubMed 30414050](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30414050/)). A 2025-era student study prompted its own authors to warn against "heals" claims ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/listening-to-specific-music-frequencies-alters-stress-markers-in-students-facing-exams/)). The researchers found no peer-reviewed support for "DNA repair."

The 432 Hz story mixes real history with myth. Italy adopted A=432 in 1881 on Verdi's advice. A 1939 London conference recommended A=440, which ISO adopted in 1955 and formalised as ISO 16 in 1975 ([Wikipedia: A440](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A440_(pitch_standard)); [Gribenski 2020](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_3195091_10/component/file_3345554/gribenski_2020_plenty.pdf)). The claim that the Nazis imposed 440 traces to a 1988 advocacy article and has no contemporary documentary support. Historian Jeffrey Herf dismisses it ([Jerusalem Post](https://www.jpost.com/international/did-nazis-change-the-universal-music-pitch-to-use-against-their-enemies-675733)). The health studies are pilots, mostly from one Florence group:

- 33 participants with a slight heart-rate drop ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/can-tuning-music-to-432hz-really-heal-you-scientists-explain-the-viral-trend/)).
- 12 spinal-injury patients with improved sleep scores ([Acta Biomedica](https://mattioli1885journals.com/index.php/actabiomedica/article/view/10755)).
- An emergency-nurse pilot concluding that 432 Hz "may help" ([PMC9534204](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9534204)).

The best-designed trial, a 42-patient dental RCT, found that **both 432 and 440 Hz music lowered anxiety versus no music** ([PMC7213780](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7213780/)). In that trial, music did most of the work, not the tuning. The tuning gap is **31.8 cents** [DERIVED], about the size of the 33-cent drift that went unnoticed even by absolute-pitch listeners in Hedger et al. (2013) ([APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/perfect-pitch-may-not-be-absolute-after-all.html)).

The Schumann resonance is real physics with sham-medicine marketing attached. It consists of lightning-excited electromagnetic standing waves in the Earth–ionosphere cavity. The fundamental sits near 7.8 Hz, with modes near 14, 20, 27 and 34 Hz that drift with ionospheric conditions ([Wikipedia: Schumann resonances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schumann_resonances)). Big Think summarises it as "Amazing physics, sham medicine" ([Big Think](https://bigthink.com/?p=454830)). It is not sound. An EQ band at 7.83 Hz touches only subsonic content. The only honest audio use is as a thematic **modulation rate**, such as a 7.83 Hz LFO.

Binaural beats have the strongest evidence of the four, and it is still mixed. A 2019 meta-analysis of 22 studies found a medium overall effect (g = 0.45) on cognition, anxiety and pain, with high heterogeneity ([PubMed 30073406](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30073406/)). A 2023 systematic review found that **5 EEG studies supported entrainment, 8 contradicted it and 1 was mixed** ([PMC10198548](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10198548)). The illusion needs headphones and works best with carriers below about 1000 Hz (some report up to 1500 Hz). Beat rates above roughly 30 Hz are heard as roughness rather than as a beat ([PMC4428073](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4428073)). A static EQ cannot create one.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solfeggio 174–963 Hz | Puleo/Horowitz numerology, 1999 | None for function | Emphasise a region; label it | High (modern, unrelated to Guido) |
| 528 Hz "love/DNA" | Horowitz-era marketing | n=9 pilot, whole-piece retune | Nothing tested; bell ≠ retune | High (evidence weak) |

## An EQ moves arousal reliably and valence weakly

The research supports a cue-based model of musical emotion. Tempo and mode carry most of the weight, and loudness tracks arousal ([Corrigall & Schellenberg 2013](https://sites.utm.utoronto.ca/sites/sites.utm.utoronto.ca.glenn_website/files/download/CorrigallSchellenberg2013.pdf); [PMC3726864](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3726864)). Timbre is a real but probabilistic contributor ([Eerola et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00487/pdf)). An EQ only controls timbre, specifically the long-term spectrum. It shapes a melody's feeling without dictating it.

Within timbre, three semantic dimensions recur across languages: **luminance** (bright–dull), **texture** (smooth–rough) and **mass** (thick–thin) [RESEARCH] ([Zacharakis, Pastiadis & Reiss 2014](https://joshreiss.github.io/documents/2014/Zacharakis%20Pastiadis%20Reiss%20-%20Music%20Perception.pdf)). Producers' own vocabulary is dominated by two words. In the SAFE crowdsourced dataset, "warm" and "bright" were the top EQ descriptors, with 440 and 424 instances ([De Man et al.](https://qmro.qmul.ac.uk/xmlui/bitstream/123456789/22150/1/De%20Man%20Semantic%20description%20of%20timbral%202016%20Accepted.pdf)). Agreement on what those words mean is weak. In SocialEQ, "warm" was the most popular term but scored only **0.139 on agreement** (Medium; via search summary) ([Cartwright & Pardo 2014](https://interactiveaudiolab.github.io/assets/papers/cartwright_pardo_collintel_2014.pdf)). Any "feeling" preset will therefore land differently across listeners and source material. The right design is a starting curve plus a single "amount" macro, not a fixed prescription.

The most dependable lever is the spectral centroid. Perceived brightness correlates with the raw centroid at **r = 0.513**, and correlates with the centroid relative to the fundamental at essentially zero ([Schubert & Wolfe 2006](https://www.phys.unsw.edu.au/jw/reprints/SchubertWolfe06.pdf)). Brightness is therefore about absolute frequency region, not harmonic alignment with the note. Eerola, Ferrer and Alluri (2012) predicted **50–57% of valence and energy-arousal ratings** for 110 instrument sounds from a handful of features: the high-to-low energy ratio, attack slope and spectral flux ([JYX PDF](https://jyx.jyu.fi/bitstream/123456789/48672/1/eerolatetal49.full.pdf)). A 2017 follow-up found that brighter sounds raise both energy arousal and *tension*, while positive valence tracks spectral slope and strong partials more than brightness alone ([Frontiers 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00153/full)). Context can erase these effects: in one continuous-rating study of a full piece, centroid had no overall influence on valence or arousal ([Heng et al. 2026, citing Dean & Bailes](https://www.mcgill.ca/mpcl/files/mpcl/heng_2026_muspercept.pdf)). The practical reading is that **darkening a melody lowers arousal fairly reliably, while EQ's effect on "happy vs sad" is weak**. Boosting 2–5 kHz raises energy but can lower valence by adding tension.

Ear physiology sharpens this. The outer ear peaks in sensitivity around **2.7–4 kHz** (ear-canal resonance), with a secondary concha peak near 5 kHz ([HyperPhysics](https://hyperphysics.gsu.edu/hbase/Sound/maxsens.html); [Goode, ORL Zürich](https://www.orl.uzh.ch/memro/program/3.9_Goode.pdf)). Small boosts there carry outsized perceptual weight. ISO 226:2023 differs from the 2003 equal-loudness contours by at most 0.6 dB ([Suzuki et al. 2024](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ast/45/1/45_e23.66/_article)). The old trap still applies: a "warm" preset tuned loud will sound thin at background level.

Two further findings decide how "frequency" presets should be built. First, **peaks are far more audible than equal-sized dips** (Bücklein 1981) ([AES e-library 10291](https://www.aes.org/e-lib/browse.cfm?elib=10291)). A broad Q=1 resonance at 5 kHz is detectable at about 0.25 dB in pink noise (Toole & Olive, via secondary summary, Medium) ([Audioholics](https://www.audioholics.com/room-acoustics/human-hearing-amplitude-sensitivity-part-1)). Second, the auditory filter bandwidth follows ERB = 24.7(4.37f + 1) ([CCRMA](https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~jos/sasp/Equivalent_Rectangular_Bandwidth.html)). A bell narrower than one ERB works out to roughly Q > 4 at 200 Hz and Q > 9 above 1 kHz [DERIVED]. A bell that narrow is heard as a pitched resonance, not a tonal colour. A narrow boost at a fixed frequency also only "lights up" when a note or harmonic lands on it. A 165 Hz, Q=24 change is obvious on matching bass notes and much less obvious otherwise ([RealTraps](https://realtraps.com/art_narrow.htm)).

The researchers did not draw out one consequence for the spiritual-frequency concept: **all nine Solfeggio numbers sit between 174 and 963 Hz** [DERIVED]. Engineering cheat sheets label that region fullness, mud (roughly 250–500 Hz) and honk (450 Hz–1 kHz) ([iZotope EQ cheat sheet](https://www.izotope.com/en/learn/eq-cheat-sheet); [Guitar Center](https://www.guitarcenter.com/riffs/gear-tips/recording/eq-cheat-sheet-for-mixing)). These band boundaries are practice-derived, not lab-validated (Medium as consensus, Low as precise limits). None of the nine reaches the 2–5 kHz presence zone or the 10 kHz+ air zone, which drive brightness and arousal most strongly. A literal "boost 396 Hz" preset therefore tends to add mud, and a literal "boost 528 Hz" preset tends to add honk. The musical alignment is also off. 528 Hz sits **15.6 cents sharp of C5** (523.25 Hz at A440), and 432 Hz sits 31.8 cents flat of A4 [DERIVED]. (One research note gave +31 cents for 528 Hz; the correct figure is 15.6.) A narrow band at either frequency falls between semitones in standard-tuned music. Producers who want "528 Hz music" set A4 = 444 Hz so that C5 lands at 528.01 Hz ([Anaamaly](https://anaamalymusic.com/page/tuning-music-432-hz-528-hz); [Toni Mazzotti](https://tonimazzotti.com/how-to-make-music-with-precise-temperament-tunings-for-432-hz-and-528-hz/)).

The resulting design rule (Medium confidence, synthesised in the notes from the thresholds above and engineering practice) has three parts:

- Implement any frequency-named emphasis as **broad (Q ≤ about 1.5) and gentle (≤ about 2–3 dB)**, so it reads as regional timbre and stays key-independent.
- Alternatively, offer a **key/tuning-aware snap** that moves narrow bands onto the song's tonic or fifth (and octaves) in the selected reference tuning.
- Build the actual "feeling" from tilt and shelves.

Octave stacking offers a bridge. The fourth octave of 528 Hz is 4224 Hz (presence) and that of 639 Hz is 10,224 Hz (air) [DERIVED]. A preset can therefore honour a Solfeggio number while working in perceptually potent regions. No study has tested chroma-targeted EQ of this kind, so it is a hypothesis.

| Intended feeling | Research axis | Starting curve (inferred, not validated) |
|---|---|---|
| Calm / warm | Low luminance, low arousal | Tilt −0.5 to −1.5 dB/oct about 700 Hz–1 kHz; gentle high-shelf cut above 6–8 kHz; keep 2–5 kHz neutral |
| Grounded | Added mass without mud | Pultec-style low shelf +40–80 Hz with a cut around 150–300 Hz |
| Clear / uplifting | Higher luminance, less mass | Broad cut 200–500 Hz; tilt +0.5 to +1.5 dB/oct; avoid a 2–5 kHz peak |
| Airy / ethereal | High luminance, low mass | High shelf 10–12 kHz, +1 to +4 dB, Q about 0.7; low-shelf cut −1 to −3 dB below about 150 Hz |
| Intimate | Mild presence, soft top | +1–2 dB at 2.5–4 kHz (Q about 0.8); mild shelf cut above 10 kHz; small cut near 300 Hz |

Colour presets should stay within about **±0.5–4 dB at Q 0.3–1.2**. Narrow boosts are the main source of "honky, ringy, cheap" artefacts ([psychoacoustics synthesis; Olive et al.](https://aes2.org/publications/elibrary-page/?id=7868)). Above all, every preset must be **loudness-matched**, or listeners will hear "louder" as "better." ITU-R BS.1770-5 (11/2023) K-weighted loudness is the defensible basis ([ITU-R BS.1770-5](https://www.itu.int/rec/R-REC-BS.1770-5-202311-I)). Because K-weighting emphasises the highs, it removes the "brighter = louder = better" confound automatically.

## A modern EQ core needs matched filters, an SVF and smoothing

The magnitude reference is the RBJ Audio EQ Cookbook, now a W3C Working Group Note dated 8 June 2021 [S] ([W3C Audio EQ Cookbook](https://www.w3.org/TR/audio-eq-cookbook)). Its peaking boost and cut at the same Q are exact inverses [C]. That makes "+g band then −g band = identity" a valid null test, and a shelf reaches exactly half its dB gain at f0 [C].

Three numerical traps matter for a commercial product:

1. **Float precision at low frequencies.** With coefficients rounded to float32, a 20 Hz, Q=4, +6 dB bell at 192 kHz deviates by **up to 2.27 dB** and shifts its peak to 19.3 Hz [C]. The fix is double-precision coefficients and state, or a topology-preserving SVF whose `g = tan(πf/fs)` keeps precision as f approaches 0 ([EarLevel](https://www.earlevel.com/main/2012/11/26/biquad-c-source-code/)).
2. **Bilinear "cramping" near Nyquist.** At 44.1 kHz, a 16 kHz, Q=1, +6 dB bell comes out **4.23 dB short** of its analog prototype at 20 kHz. A 10 kHz, Q=0.7, +12 dB bell reaches only 0.51 dB at 20 kHz where the analog prototype gives 5.75 dB [C]. At 96 kHz the error falls below about 1.3 dB [C]. Fixes include Orfanidis's prescribed-Nyquist-gain design ([AES 7854](https://secure.aes.org/forum/pubs/journal/?elib=7854)) and Vicanek's closed-form matched biquads (2016) and matched shelves (2024) ([Vicanek 2016](https://www.vicanek.de/articles/BiquadFits.pdf); [Vicanek 2024](https://www.vicanek.de/articles/2poleShelvingFits.pdf)). These add zero latency and cost only a little extra math per coefficient update, so they are the best-value fix for "air" presets at 44.1/48 kHz, which most sessions still use.
3. **Modulation clicks.** Direct-form biquad states become inconsistent when coefficients jump. Andrew Simper's trapezoidal SVF has the same response as an RBJ filter but stays smooth even under audio-rate modulation (author's claim, widely repeated, Medium-High) ([Cytomic SvfLinearTrapOptimised2](https://cytomic.com/files/dsp/SvfLinearTrapOptimised2.pdf)). JUCE's built-in `StateVariableTPTFilter` offers only low-, band- and high-pass, with **no bell or shelf modes** [S] ([JUCE dsp docs](https://docs.juce.com/master/group__juce__dsp-processors.html)). The developer must write a custom SVF bell/shelf class from Simper's mixing coefficients.

No verified method combines SVF behaviour with Vicanek-style Nyquist matching. That tradeoff remains open.

Real-time hygiene follows standard JUCE practice:

- Smooth frequency in log, gain in dB and Q in log over about 20–50 ms.
- Snap smoothers on preset recall so loading a preset does not audibly sweep.
- Never call `Coefficients::make*` in `processBlock`, because it heap-allocates (forum, Medium) ([JUCE forum](https://forum.juce.com/t/dsp-noise-glitches-when-automating-frequency-via-slow-lfo/63949)).
- Read parameters through APVTS atomics and wrap processing in `ScopedNoDenormals` ([juce_dsp.h](https://github.com/juce-framework/JUCE/blob/master/modules/juce_dsp/juce_dsp.h)).

Two product features have measurable pitfalls. A plain dry/wet knob on a minimum-phase EQ misbehaves. A 50% mix of a +12 dB bell peaks at **+7.9 dB**, not +6 dB. A 50% mix of a 24 dB/oct high-pass at 100 Hz carves a **−12.9 dB cancellation notch at 109 Hz** [C]. The "amount" macro should therefore scale every band's dB gain, as Pro-Q's global scale does, rather than crossfade dry and wet. Auto-gain should default to a *static* estimate computed from the curve, weighted over a pink or K-weighted spectrum. FabFilter describes its own approach as "an educated guess based on the current EQ settings" ([FabFilter Pro-Q help](https://fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/output)). Signal-adaptive gain riding draws user complaints about pumping ([KVR](https://kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=7547360)).

The other recommendations are:

- Ship minimum-phase as the default.
- Offer linear phase as an option, built from the analog magnitude so it has no cramping, with latency reported and a worker-thread kernel rebuild. Its cost is pre-ringing on transients ([SoundGym](https://www.soundgym.co/blog/item?id=linear-phase-eq-pre-ringing)).
- Treat FabFilter's "Natural Phase" as proprietary. Its method is unpublished ([FabFilter Pro-Q 4 Help](https://www.fabfilter.com/help/pro-q/using/processingmode)).
- For the analyser, use a Hann-windowed FFT of 4096–8192 points fed through a lock-free FIFO off the audio thread. JUCE's own tutorial has known data races ([JUCE forum](https://forum.juce.com/t/data-race-in-spectrum-analyser-tutorial/65715)).
- Gate releases on pluginval: strictness 5 is the community minimum and 10 the release check ([pluginval README](https://github.com/Tracktion/pluginval/blob/master/README.md)). Add analytic magnitude tests at every sample rate and bit-exact flat-EQ nulls.

## The niche has retuners and tone generators but no EQ

The existing "healing frequency" plugin market is crowded at the cheap end and empty in the middle. Its products fall into three groups:

- **Pitch-shifting retuners.** Spiritual Tuner (€14.90–$27.45; the sources conflict) ([KVR](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/spiritual-tuner-solfeggio-frequency-tuner-by-spiritual-audio-labs)). Atix Universe's RASTA 528Hz and 432Hz MASTER, the latter calling 440 Hz "blurry and unnatural" ([KVR](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/432hz-master-by-atix-universe)). touski's HertzBloom ($10, with a free tier) ([KVR marketplace](https://www.kvraudio.com/marketplace/hertzbloom---432-hz-reference-pitch-plugin-vst3-and-au-by-touski)). Kushview's **free, open-source reTuner** (AU/VST3/CLAP/LV2), released around December 2025 and framed neutrally as a tuning tool ([Bedroom Producers Blog](https://bedroomproducersblog.com/2025/12/15/kushview-retuner/)).
- **Tone, binaural and Solfeggio generators.** These are largely free and dated, led by SaschArt's catalogue ([SaschArt](https://vst.saschart.com/binauralgenerator_monaural_beats)).
- **Solfeggio pad synths.** A free six-tone Windows VST ([KVR](https://www.kvraudio.com/product/ancient-solfeggio-synthesizer-by-ulusulu-music)) and Om Infinite Sounds' nine-voice synth ([Pluginomat](https://pluginomat.com/product/solfeggio-by-om-infinite-sounds/)).

**No parametric EQ with Solfeggio or 432 presets surfaced** on KVR, Plugin Boutique or the other sources searched (Medium confidence; KVR pages were read via search summaries only). Most competitors are single-developer, often Windows-only or legacy, and lean on "528 Hz repairs DNA" copy.

Demand is real but sits in wellness content rather than mix engineering. Meditative Mind, the largest healing-frequency channel, had about **6.49 million subscribers and 1.8 billion views** as of 3 November 2026 ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/youtube-stats/channel/UCM0YvsRfYfsniGAhjvYFOSA)). "528 Hz Love Frequency" by Miracle Tones shows about **177 million Spotify streams** on a third-party counter ([mystreamcount](https://www.mystreamcount.com/track/4ovQlgSLQsDpmE4SplH10J)). These are snapshots; no trend data could be retrieved. Producers do not EQ-boost these frequencies. They set DAW tuning to A4 = 432 (or 444 for "C528"), pitch-shift finished audio, and layer pure tones or binaural beds ([Solfeggio Frequencies.org](https://solfeggiofrequencies.org/4-ways-to-re-tune-music-to-432hz-and-528hz/); [Royalty Free Music Hub](https://royaltyfreemusichub.com/tune-your-daw-or-keyboard-to-solfeggio/)). Many tracks labelled "528 Hz" mix up key and tuning and are unverified ([Frequency Generator Blog](https://www.frequencysoundgenerator.com/blog/healing-frequencies-432hz-528hz/)). The likely buyer is therefore a wellness creator whose real pain is *proving* a track is correctly tuned. That points to a tuning readout or exportable tuning tag as a feature, plus a combined retune, drone and EQ workflow that no single product currently offers (inference).

The mainstream benchmark sets user expectations. FabFilter Pro-Q 4 ($199 list) offers up to 24 bands, dynamic and spectral-dynamic bands, EQ Match, Spectrum Grab, an instance list, Gentle/Warm character modes and Tilt Shelf/Flat Tilt shapes ([Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/fabfilter-pro-q-4-expert-review); [Plugin Boutique](https://www.pluginboutique.com/product/2-Effects/16-EQ/14125-FabFilter-Pro-Q-4)). The free TDR Nova sets the floor for dynamic EQ ([TDR Nova](https://www.tokyodawn.net/tdr-nova/free/)). Character EQs sell one memorable move, and each maps onto a spiritual-preset UX:

- The Pultec boost/cut low end ([Wikipedia: Pultec EQP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pultec_EQP-1)).
- The Maag EQ4 Air Band, a boost-only shelf with stepped corners at 2.5–40 kHz ([Maag manual](https://maag.audio/user_manual/EQ4_500_User_Manual.pdf)). A Maag-style stepped one-knob band could step through Solfeggio corners.
- Target-profile EQs: smart:EQ 4 at about $89 ([sonible](https://www.sonible.com/smarteq4/)) and Ozone's Tonal Balance Control ([iZotope](https://www.izotope.com/products/ozone-advanced)). A "tonal profile" target could become a spiritual preset family.

Indie EQ prices cluster at about **$29–149**, below the $199 premium anchor. In Moonbase's 2025 survey of 95 companies, **49% said prices are falling** ([Moonbase report](https://moonbase.sh/reports/state-of-audio-plugin-companies-2025/)). The notes infer a list price of about $39–79 with $19–29 launch pricing. That is a judgement, not sourced niche data.

The community reaction is the strongest market signal. KVR users dismiss "frequency magic nonsense" while accepting 432 Hz as a legitimate, arbitrary reference pitch ([KVR](https://www.kvraudio.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=8925865)). Splice calls 432 a "small but vocal minority" choice ([Splice](https://splice.com/blog/debunking-432-hz-tuning-standard)). The backlash targets claims, not tools: neutrally framed reTuner draws none of it.

## JUCE 9, VST3 under MIT and AU cover most DAWs cheaply

The tooling picture changed materially in 2025–26:

- **JUCE is on version 9** (tag 9.0.6, bundling AAX SDK 2.10) [primary, verified] ([JUCE CHANGE_LIST](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/juce-framework/JUCE/master/CHANGE_LIST.md)). It was announced around 21 July 2026 ([KVR](https://www.kvraudio.com/news/juce-9-now-available-67802)).

## Health claims need clinical trials the field does not have

US law sets a high bar. The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance (20 December 2022) applies to *all* health-related claims, not only supplements. It requires "competent and reliable scientific evidence," which for health benefits generally means **randomised, controlled, ideally double-blind human trials on the product itself**. Animal and in-vitro work is generally insufficient ([FTC guidance PDF](https://ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Health-Products-Compliance-Guidance.pdf); [Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2023/01/ftc-issues-new-guidance-on-health-related-claims-to-replace-the-dietary-supplements-advertising-guide)). Traditional-use claims need an explicit statement that no scientific evidence exists. The FTC's own example is an herbal tea labelled "Ancient remedy … There is no scientific evidence that it works." A disclaimer does not rescue an express health claim. The FTC has acted against software and audio products on this basis: the Ultimeyes vision app, the Aura "Instant Blood Pressure" app, the Mole Detective apps, and the MSA 30X sound amplifier's "hear up to 30 times better" ([FTC blog](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2016/12/app-developer-under-pressure-deceptive-health-claims); [FTC press release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2018/05/ftc-settlement-turns-down-volume-deceptive-sound-amplifier-ads)). **No FTC action specifically against binaural or Solfeggio audio was found** (Medium; the case database could not be searched directly).

The FDA's General Wellness policy (2016, revised 2019, reissued 6 January 2026, Medium on the reissue details) tolerates "promote relaxation" and "manage stress" claims for low-risk products. FDA's own example is a music app that helps users "soothe and relax." The policy excludes any reference to disease, including anxiety disorders and insomnia ([FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/99809/download)). It is a device-regulation safe harbour, not an exemption from FTC truthfulness rules. In the UK, the ASA "has yet to see any convincing evidence that any form of healing therapy can treat physical or mental medical conditions" ([ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/health-healing-therapy.html)). The ASA ruled against a bio-resonance "frequency" device on substantiation grounds ([ASA: Navi Organics](https://www.asa.org.uk/rulings/navi-organics-ltd-a24-1272405-navi-organics-ltd.html)). EU and UK consumer-protection statutes also bar misleading health claims. The researchers could not retrieve their primary texts, so specific provisions are not cited here.

| Risk | Example wording | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Low | "Presets named after the popular modern Solfeggio and 432 Hz numbers"; "warm / airy / grounded tonal curves"; "for creative sound-shaping"; "no therapeutic effect is claimed" | Descriptive, aesthetic, historical |
| Moderate | "Relaxing presets"; "design calm-sounding mixes"; "meditation-ready sound" | Keep it about the *sound's character* or creative intent, not the listener's body |
| High | "Reduces stress/cortisol"; "improves sleep"; "heals"; "repairs DNA"; "entrains brainwaves"; "clinically proven"; "tuned to Earth's frequency"; citing Akimoto or Calamassi as proof; outcome testimonials; disease words (insomnia, anxiety disorder, tinnitus) | Needs RCT substantiation that does not exist |

An EQ marketed for music production is not a medical device. Exposure arises only through how it is advertised. The practical regulators are the FTC, the ASA/CAP and consumer-protection authorities, along with store and platform policies.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the product. The plugin cannot deliver frequency-specific feelings. It can deliver **timbre-specific feelings that carry spiritual names honestly**. The "spiritual" layer works best as a *tuning and labelling* layer: snapping bands to in-tune pitches at A=432 or A=444, octave-stacking a Solfeggio number up into the presence or air regions where it audibly matters, a thematic 7.83 Hz modulation rate, and an optional headphone-only binaural bed with carriers under 1 kHz. The *emotional* layer comes from established psychoacoustics: broad tilts, gentle shelves, peak-averse curves and strict loudness matching. One derived point matters for presets: every Solfeggio number falls in the muddy-to-honky low midrange. Taking the numbers literally would produce worse-sounding presets, so translating them into region-appropriate curves is a sound-quality requirement as much as a compliance one.

Strategically, the empty middle of the market and the hostility of the mainstream audio community point the same way. A well-engineered, cross-platform EQ that wellness creators can use and sceptical engineers will not mock does not exist yet. The kind of copy that drew KVR's ridicule is also what the FTC and ASA would act on. An in-plugin "about the science" panel, a verifiable tuning readout and copy built from the low-risk column above could turn the category's credibility problem into the product's main differentiator. Before launch, the developer should re-verify the facts the researchers could only see through search summaries: JUCE EULA terms, Avid/PACE signing costs, CLAP host support and the exact FDA 2026 text.
